- age (years)

We'll assume that "income per dependent" and "monthly credit card expenses" are continuous variables, "age" is also a continuous variable, "owning a home" and "self employed" are categorical variables, and "number of derogatory reports" can be considered as an ordinal variable.

For simplicity, let's consider the target variable (credit application acceptance) as binary: accepted or not accepted.

### Step 1: Feature Selection
The most important features for credit application decisions typically include:
- **Income**: Higher income generally increases creditworthiness.
- **Credit History (Derogatory Reports)**: Fewer derogatory reports indicate better credit history and higher likelihood of approval.
- **Age**: Often, older individuals have established credit histories, but this can vary widely among individuals.

### Step 2: Tree Construction
With a maximum depth of 2, our tree will have one root node and up to four leaf nodes. We prioritize features based on general relevance to creditworthiness.

**Decision Tree:**

|--- income per dependent <= 3
||--- number of derogatory reports <= 1
|||--- class: accepted
||--- number of derogatory reports > 1
|||--- class: not accepted
|--- income per dependent > 3
||--- age >= 30
|||--- class: accepted
||--- age < 30
|||--- class: not accepted

**Rationale:**
- **Income per Dependent**: This is a critical factor in determining creditworthiness, as higher income generally means better ability to repay debts.
- **Number of Derogatory Reports**: For lower-income applicants, the number of derogatory reports becomes a crucial factor. Even with lower income, having no or few derogatory reports can indicate responsible financial behavior.
- **Age**: For those with higher incomes, age can serve as an additional indicator of stability and creditworthiness. Older individuals might have had more time to establish good credit.

This tree is simplified and based on assumptions about the importance of features in credit decisions. In real-world applications, more complex decision trees or other machine learning models, combined with thorough feature engineering and selection, would be used to achieve higher accuracy.

Here's a Python function representing the decision tree with a maximum depth of 2 for predicting whether an application for credit is accepted or not. The thresholds used are based on general assumptions about creditworthiness and may need adjustments according to specific data.

```python
def predict_credit_application(X: dict):
    nodes = 2 * [None]
    nodes[0] = X["Income.per.dependent"] <= 5  # Assuming higher income per dependent increases creditworthiness
    nodes[1] = X["Derogatory.reports"] <= 1  # Fewer derogatory reports indicate better credit history
    
    if nodes[0]:
        prediction = 0  # Lower income per dependent, likely not accepted
    else:
        if nodes[1]:
            prediction = 1  # Higher income and good credit history, likely accepted
        else:
            prediction = 0  # Higher income but poor credit history, likely not accepted
    
    return prediction, [int(node) for node in nodes]
```

In this function:
- `X` is a dictionary containing the features.
- The first node checks if the `Income.per.dependent` is less than or equal to 5. This threshold is chosen assuming that higher income per dependent generally increases creditworthiness.
- The second node checks if the number of `Derogatory.reports` is less than or equal to 1, indicating a good credit history.
- Based on these conditions, the function predicts whether the application for credit is accepted (1) or not (0).
- The function also returns a list of boolean values representing the truth values of the inner nodes. `True` is represented as 1 and `False` as 0.

You can use this function by passing a dictionary with the feature values, like so:

